Protein language models and related variant effect predictors (VEPs) are increasingly used to classify missense variants, yet their reliability is typically summarized by benchmark-wide averages. In practice, predictors are applied to proteins that are evolutionarily distant from those used for calibration, which constitutes a family-level distribution shift. Standard split conformal prediction assumes exchangeable variants and offers no guarantee when entire protein families are unseen, while existing VEP calibration schemes condition on residue, gene or domain attributes rather than on homology support.
We present ProtCOVER, a conformal procedure that treats homology families, rather than individual variants, as the exchangeable unit. ProtCOVER (i) constructs label-free homology clusters and calibrates at the family level, yielding marginal coverage for a new family under family exchangeability; (ii) reweights calibration families using estimated family-level density ratios from label-free descriptors (sequence similarity, alignment depth and embedding distance) to adapt to descriptor shift; and (iii) computes the effective calibration support for each query protein and returns a conservative fallback set, or abstains, when support is insufficient. We state the assumptions under which each guarantee holds and separately measure empirical robustness where they do not.
We evaluate ProtCOVER on the ProteinGym clinical substitution benchmark using precomputed zero-shot scores from five predictors under leave-family-out splits, against split, Mondrian, group-weighted and hierarchical conformal baselines. Class-conditional split conformal, calibrated over variants, covers only \sOneBtwoPath{} of pathogenic variants per family for four of the five predictors at a 0.90 target, even without shift. Under descriptor shift, family-level calibration alone falls to \worstHcp{}, while ProtCOVER's family-level weighting keeps family-weighted pathogenic and benign coverage at or above \worstPw{} for every predictor and shifted setting, at the cost of larger prediction sets. Its gains come from shifts in alignment depth and sequence similarity; on orphan families and on held-out regions of embedding space it brings no gain over the group-weighted and family-level baselines. The support gate preserves these guarantees, but on this benchmark support-based abstention did not reduce the error of retained predictions relative to random abstention.
