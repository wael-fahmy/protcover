\section{Introduction}

Zero-shot variant effect predictors (VEPs), from protein language models such as ESM-1b~\cite{rives2021biological,brandes2023genome} to alignment-based models such as EVE and GEMME~\cite{frazer2021disease,laine2019gemme} and hybrids such as TranceptEVE and PoET~\cite{notin2022trancepteve,truong2023poet}, now rank missense variants with high accuracy on clinical benchmarks~\cite{notin2023proteingym}. Clinical use, however, needs more than a ranking: variant interpretation under the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP)~\cite{richards2015standards} needs evidence whose error rate is known, and the ClinGen calibration of computational evidence~\cite{pejaver2022calibration} has shown that a single genome-wide calibration can hide large differences between genes~\cite{tejura2024calibration}.

Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023gentle} offers distribution-free error control: a prediction set over $\{\text{benign},\text{pathogenic}\}$ that contains the true label with probability at least $1-\alpha$. Its guarantee rests on exchangeability between calibration and test data. For VEPs this assumption fails in two ways. First, variants are grouped into proteins and homologous protein families: a predictor's accuracy differs from family to family, and families contribute very different numbers of labeled variants (from \minVar{} to \maxVar{} per protein in our data). Pooling variants lets the largest families set the error rate, so the ``90\%'' is a statement about variants from well-studied genes, not about a new protein. Second, the proteins a predictor is deployed on are often evolutionarily distant from, or have shallower alignments than, the proteins in the calibration set: a family-level distribution shift that is visible without labels.

We make three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Problem and evidence.} We formalize \emph{family-weighted} coverage, the probability that a random variant of a new protein family is covered, and its class-conditional version for pathogenic variants, and show that standard calibration misses it. On ProteinGym clinical substitutions, class-conditional (Mondrian) split conformal, the standard remedy for class imbalance, meets its own variant-level target yet covers only \sOneBtwoPath{} of pathogenic variants per family for four of five predictors at a 0.90 target, with no distribution shift at all (\sFourBtwoPathNT{} on held-out regions of protein space). Family-level calibration removes this failure when families are exchangeable and greatly reduces it under shift.
\item \emph{Method and guarantees.} ProtCOVER (i) calibrates at the family level, (ii) reweights calibration families by a cross-fitted density ratio of label-free family descriptors, and (iii) abstains when calibration support is insufficient. Its new formal result is Theorem~\ref{thm:weighted}, a finite-sample guarantee for family-weighted coverage under family-level covariate shift, obtained by placing the density-ratio weights and the test mass on whole families: it joins the family pooling of hierarchical conformal prediction~\cite{lee2026hierarchical} (our Proposition~\ref{prop:hcp}) with the weighted exchangeability of~\cite{tibshirani2019conformal}. We bound the loss from estimated, cross-fitted weights (Corollary~\ref{cor:estimated}); the gate only enlarges sets, so it preserves these marginal guarantees (Lemma~\ref{lem:enlarge}).
\item \emph{Evaluation, including where the method does not help.} We test five predictors on four kinds of split (exchangeable, shifted descriptors, orphan families, held-out embedding regions). Over the \nShiftCells{} shifted predictor-setting cells, ProtCOVER has the smallest average shortfall of its worse class below the target (\shortPwMean{}, against \shortGwcpMean{} for group-weighted conformal prediction and \shortHcpMean{} for family-level calibration alone) and the best worst case (\worstPw{}, against \worstGwcp{} and \worstHcp{}), at the cost of larger sets. The gain comes from shifts in alignment depth and sequence similarity; on orphan families and held-out embedding regions weighting brings no gain over these baselines, and support-based abstention does not beat random abstention.
\end{itemize}
